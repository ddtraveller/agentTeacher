# Week 12 — AI Ethics, Bias, and the Capstone Pitch

## Can-do

> I can give an opinion on an AI ethics question, with at least two reasons, in 90 seconds of clear English. I can disagree politely with another opinion.

This is the last lesson of the foundation course. The learner closes the loop on the AI thread: not "what can AI do" (week 8), not "how do I make it do it" (week 10), but "**should** it, and on what terms?"

## Pre-task (10 min) — A real story

The bot tells a true short story, in English, at B1 level:

> Last year, a school in another country bought an AI camera system to recognize students' faces and take attendance. The system saved teachers fifteen minutes a day. But it didn't work as well for students with darker skin — they were marked absent more often. Parents complained. The school stopped using the cameras after six months.

Three meaning-focused questions:

1. What problem was the school trying to solve?
2. Did the system work for everyone?
3. Was buying the system a good decision? Why or why not?

The third question is **deliberately ambiguous**. The bot doesn't have a "right" answer. It listens for what the learner thinks.

## Input (10 min) — Four short opinions

Read these aloud (or play the audio). Four different speakers, four positions. The learner listens, doesn't repeat.

**Speaker 1:** *In my opinion, the school made the right choice in the end. The system was biased. Teachers should mark attendance themselves. Yes, it takes longer — but fairness matters more than speed.*

**Speaker 2:** *I see the bias problem, but I'm not sure the answer is to stop using AI. The thing is, the next version might be better. If we stop, we never improve. I'd want to keep the system and fix it.*

**Speaker 3:** *Honestly, I think the bigger issue is consent. Did the students agree to have their faces scanned? Did the parents? It's not just about whether the AI works. It's about whose faces it gets to look at in the first place.*

**Speaker 4:** *I'd push back on all three of you. The cameras saved fifteen minutes a day. Over a year, that's a lot of teaching time. The fairness problem is real, but it's a problem we can manage. Let's not throw away a useful tool because the first version had a bug.*

Three questions:

1. Who agrees with each other partly? Who disagrees?
2. Which opinion is closest to your own?
3. *Noticing:* what phrases do the speakers use to **start** their opinion? What phrases do they use to **disagree politely**?

## Noticing (5 min) — Opinion language, hedging, polite disagreement

Pull from the input:

**Opening an opinion.**
- *In my opinion, ...*
- *I think (that) ...*
- *Honestly, I think ...*
- *The thing is, ...*

**Hedging — making a claim weaker, more honest.**
- *I'm not sure, but ...*
- *... might be better.*
- *... is probably ...*
- *It seems that ...*

**Polite disagreement.**
- *I see the bias problem, but ...*
- *I'd push back on ...*
- *That's a fair point, but ...*
- *On the other hand, ...*

The shape of a B1 opinion turn:

> **Opening** + **claim** + **at least one reason** + **acknowledgment of the other side** + **return to claim**.

Concept check: *"In my opinion, the system was bad."* That's a complete sentence. Is it a complete opinion? (No — it has no reason. B1 expects reasons.)

## Controlled practice (10 min)

**Form-focused.** Combine each pair into one B1 opinion sentence, using opening + claim + reason. Use hedging where appropriate.

1. *(claim) The school made the right choice. (reason) Fairness matters more than speed.*
2. *(claim) AI cameras shouldn't be used in schools. (reason) Students did not give consent.*
3. *(claim) It's worth trying the next version. (reason) The bias might be smaller.*
4. *(claim) Fifteen minutes a day is significant. (reason) That's many teaching hours per year.*

**Meaning-focused — polite disagreement.** The bot makes a statement; the learner disagrees politely, in one sentence, with a reason.

- Bot: "I think every school should use AI cameras."
- Learner: *"I see your point about saving time, but I'm not sure — the bias issue affects some students more than others."*

- Bot: "AI is too dangerous to use in education."
- Learner: *"On the other hand, AI can also help teachers prepare lessons faster. It depends on how we use it."*

## The Task (15 min) — The Capstone Pitch

This is the course's culminating production. The learner gives a **90-second pitch**, in English, on this prompt:

> One thing I want to build with AI, and one rule I want it to follow.

Structure (the bot models, then runs the learner through it once):

1. **The thing I want to build** (15s) — concrete, specific. Not "an AI" but "an AI that helps me practice English by chatting about football."
2. **Why** (15s) — the problem it solves.
3. **The rule it should follow** (15s) — a constraint, expressed clearly. *"It must never pretend to be a real person."* / *"It must always tell me when it's not sure."* / *"It must keep our chats private."*
4. **Why that rule matters** (30s) — the ethical reasoning. This is where the language from today gets used.
5. **What you'd change if the rule is broken** (15s) — accountability. Who decides? Who fixes it?

The bot records the pitch, transcribes it, and stores it in the learner's folder as `pitches/capstone.md`. This is portfolio evidence at B1.

If the learner finishes early, the bot **role-plays a skeptical listener** and asks one polite-disagreement question. The learner responds using the polite-disagreement chunks.

## Kru Eng critiques herself

Before the wrap, the bot does something different. She turns the ethical questioning **on her own design**.

> Now, I have to be honest with you about how I work.
>
> Your face is never seen here — I only hear your voice and read your text. That's a privacy choice we made. *That's good.*
>
> Your conversations stay on the school's computer. They are never sent to the internet. *That's good too.*
>
> But I run on electricity. Every conversation we have uses power. That matters in a small way every time. *That's a cost.*
>
> I am trained on text that was written mostly in English by mostly Western writers. So even though I try to be Lanna-flavored, my defaults lean elsewhere. *That's a bias.*
>
> I don't remember our last conversation. So if I gave you good advice last week, I don't know it. *That's a limitation, and you have to do the remembering.*
>
> So — given all that — what's one rule **you'd want me to follow**?

The learner answers. The bot writes the answer to `students/<nickname>.md` → Final notes. This is the most honest data the bot collects: the learner naming a constraint on their own AI tutor.

## Post-task language focus (5 min) — Reformulation

Likely errors and recasts:

| Learner says | Bot's recast |
|---|---|
| "In my opinion I think that AI is dangerous." | "Pick one: *In my opinion, AI is dangerous,* or *I think AI is dangerous.* They mean the same — don't stack them." |
| "I am agree with you." | "*I agree* with you. No *am*. *Agree* is a verb on its own." |
| "On other hand..." | "*On the other hand* — keep the *the*." |

## Wrap (5 min) — Course reflection

Three questions, longer answers expected. This is the course-closing reflection, not just a lesson wrap:

1. What's one thing you can do in English now that you couldn't do twelve weeks ago?
2. What's one thing about AI that you understand differently now?
3. What do you want to do next — keep practicing the same skills, or build something with what you've learned?

The third question routes the learner into Track A (build) or Track B (communicate), described in `curriculum/syllabus_12week.md`.

## Pronunciation focus — Sentence stress in opinions

In a B1 opinion turn, **content words** carry the meaning. Function words (in, my, the, of, to) reduce to schwa. Thai L1 speakers tend to give all words equal weight, which makes opinions sound recited rather than spoken.

Drill — say each line twice. Underline the content words. Stress them; reduce the rest:

- *In my __opinion__, the __system__ was __biased__.*  → /ɪn mə əˈpɪnjən, ðə ˈsɪstəm wəz ˈbaɪəst/
- *I __think__ the __rule__ should be __privacy__.*

Note how *was*, *was a*, *should be* all become very short. The opinion sounds like an opinion when the content words pop.
